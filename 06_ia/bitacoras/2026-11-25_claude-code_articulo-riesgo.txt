    route that isn't the cheapest one. So "A* finds the minimum-risk route" is
    not true of the code.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Issue: The "fastest" baseline already includes risk
  Where: Fast mode uses (1 + 0.25*edgePhi) and GAMMA*riskField
  Why it blocks the paper: The baseline isn't time-only, so the comparison is

  The retrofit: a design the paper can defend

  1. Street graph: real OpenStreetMap walking network for alcaldía Cuauhtémoc.
     In Python, use OSMnx to download it and networkx to search it.
  2. Risk model (the learned part): use the CDMX attorney general's open data on
     victims in investigation files (FGJ "víctimas en carpetas de
     investigación", on datos.cdmx.gob.mx), filtered to robo a transeúnte. Fit a
     kernel density estimate on past incidents. Each edge's risk r(e) is the
     density integrated along the edge. The kernel bandwidth is chosen on a
     validation year, which answers "how were hyperparameters chosen."
  3. Temporal split: fit on year 1, choose the bandwidth on year 2, test on
     year 3. Measuring the route against future incidents is what turns this
     from a demo into an experiment.
  4. Cost and heuristic: c(e) = t(e) · (1 + λ·r(e)), with t(e) = length /
     walking speed. Because r ≥ 0, every edge costs at least its walking time.
     So h(n) = straight-line distance / v_max never overestimates and satisfies
     the triangle inequality. A* is then provably optimal, and that's a short
     derivation from AIMA's own A* material.
  5. The proposal's time budget: for each origin–destination (OD) pair,
     binary-search λ for the largest value whose route time stays within
     (1+ε)·T_fast. To justify the search, prove that risk never goes up as λ
     goes up, in three lines:
     - Let route 1 be optimal at λ₁ and route 2 at λ₂, with λ₁ < λ₂.
     - Optimality gives T₁+λ₁R₁ ≤ T₂+λ₁R₂ and T₂+λ₂R₂ ≤ T₁+λ₂R₁.
     - Adding the two gives (λ₂−λ₁)(R₂−R₁) ≤ 0, so R₂ ≤ R₁, and from there T₂ ≥
       T₁.
  6. Drop or ground the main-street discount and the safe spots. With no data
     behind them, they become a limitation, not a model term.

  4. Experiments
  - Setup:
    - Baseline: pure-time A* (λ = 0), the proposal's own baseline.
    - Main metric: test-year incidents within r meters of the route, per km (↓).
      Report it next to the time overhead.
    - Secondary: nodes expanded by A* vs. uniform-cost search, which is the

  - Variability: the search is deterministic, so variance comes from the OD

  - Table 1: rows Baseline / Ours (ε = 0.1) / Ours (ε = 0.2). Columns exposure

  - A kernel estimate assumes risk stays the same over time.
  - No time-of-day modeling unless you add it.
  - Stigmatizing neighborhoods and pushing foot traffic off businesses in

  - Malicious or false reports.
  - The 4-week H3 user study is separate evidence, not this metric.

  What the risk value means

  These are reported robberies per square meter. They are not the chance that
  one person walking a street gets robbed.

  Madero comes out highest partly because it's one of the busiest pedestrian
  streets in the city. More people means more robberies, even if each walker's
  own chance is low. There's no pedestrian count to divide by, so the data can't
  separate the two.

  Practical consequences:
  - What the paper can claim: routes that pass fewer reported robberies,
    measured as exposure to test-year incidents. Not "a lower chance of being
    robbed."
  - Limitations section: this goes in as the main limitation.
  - App wording: the app shouldn't claim more than that either.
  - Counterintuitive routes: the safe route will tend to avoid busy streets,
    which some people feel are safer. That needs a sentence in the discussion
    too.

● There are three levels of fix: what the app shows, how the business is
  pitched, and the model itself. The first two cost almost nothing. The third is
  the only one that fixes the cause.

  1. App display (no model change)

  - Don't show a risk map. Keep risk inside the route cost and never draw it as
    a heatmap, a colored street or a "dangerous area" label. Show the comparison
    only: "this route passes 3 fewer reported incidents and takes 4 more
    minutes." Nobody sees a map of "bad neighborhoods," and the product
    guidelines already say to show outcomes, not internals.
  - No rankings by colonia or alcaldía. The 50 m bandwidth already keeps risk at
    block level. Never add it up into a neighborhood score in the interface,
    and never present one.
  - The time budget limits avoidance by design. With ε = 0.1–0.2, a route can be
    at most 10–20% slower. The planner sidesteps a few blocks, it doesn't send
    walkers out of a whole area. This is worth one sentence in the paper,
    because it's built into the method.

  2. Business side (pitch and canvas wording)

  - Sell visibility along routes, not placement in safe zones. Hypothesis H2 and
    the canvas currently say "negocios en zonas seguras." That implicitly ranks
    neighborhoods, and it excludes businesses on high-report streets from
    paying. Reword it to businesses along walking routes.
  - Reframe open businesses as part of safety. A business open late, with lights
    and people, is part of what makes a street feel safe. That's a positive
    pitch for businesses on busy streets instead of a penalty. It's only a claim
    in the pitch, though. Without data it can't be a term in the cost (that's
    why the "safe spots" term was dropped).

  3. Model (the actual cause)

  The stigma and the counterintuitive routes have the same cause: busy streets
  look risky because more people walk them. Possible fixes, from strongest to
  weakest:

  - Normalize by pedestrian exposure. Divide incident density by a proxy for
    foot traffic, so risk becomes closer to incidents per walker. A cheap proxy
    is the density of shops and services from OpenStreetMap, or from INEGI's
    business directory (DENUE). This fixes both limitations at once. It's also a
    real change to the method: the proxy needs justifying, and the experiment
    has to be rerun.
  - Recency weighting. Older incidents count less, so a street isn't labeled
    permanently. scikit-learn's KDE takes sample weights, and the decay rate
    would be tuned on the 2025 validation year, the same way as the bandwidth.
  - Time of day. The data has the hour, so a street can be risky at 2 a.m. and
    not at noon. This removes the "this street is bad, period" label, but splits
    the data into smaller fits.
  - Report moderation (for the user-report phase): a report changes the route
    cost only after several independent confirmations, and it expires after a
    while.

  Recommendation

  - Do levels 1 and 2 now. They're wording and interface changes. The paper's
    harms paragraph can name them as mitigations already in place, once the app
    follows them.
  - Treat level 3 as a decision, not a quick fix. Exposure normalization is the
    most valuable. It's also a second risk model with its own validation, so it

  The best α is about 0.05. Past that, predictions get steadily worse. With α =

  month's robberies actually happen. Robbery hotspots in Cuauhtémoc are stable,

  know.

  Per report, the weight is already high

  The baseline is built from 5,235 incidents. A month of simulated reports is
  only about 37 points. So α = 0.05 spread over 37 reports means each report
  counts about 7 times as much as one historical incident. At α = 0.3 that would
  be about 60 times.

  So the setup you described already comes out of the data: the neighborhood
  history sets the floor, and each fresh report pushes the street's risk up well
  above it. Framed per report, the weight is also a parameter the app can use
  directly, because it doesn't depend on how many reports arrive in a month.

  Why the simulation can't justify more weight

  The real case for reports is information the FGJ data lacks: unreported
  robberies, broken lights, a hazard that appeared yesterday. Synthetic reports
  drawn from FGJ incidents can't contain that. And if I invent reports with that
  kind of information and then score routes against the same invented events,
  the simulation grades itself. A heavier weight can be a product decision, but
  this data can't show that it helps.

          +ian bump of width $h$ on each of                              
      122 +the $N$ fit incidents $x_i$:                                  
      123 +$\hat f(x) = \frac{1}{N\,2\pi h^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \exp\!\big(-\

          + it, obtaining $d(e)$, and divide by

      136 +\textbf{Search.} The cost of an edge is $c_\lambda(e) = t(e)\,
          +\big(1+\lambda\, r(e)\big)$. For a                            
      137 +route $P$, let $T(P)=\sum_{e\in P} t(e)$ be its walking time a
          +nd $R(P)=\sum_{e\in P} t(e)\,r(e)$                            
      138 +its accumulated risk, so its cost is $T(P)+\lambda R(P)$. We r
          +un A*~\cite{hart1968formal} with                              
      139 +$h(n)=g(n,B)/v$, where $g(n,B)$ is the great-circle distance f
          +rom $n$ to the destination $B$. This                          
      140 +heuristic never overestimates: an edge is at least as long as 
          +the straight line between its ends,                           
      141 +so $c_\lambda(e) \ge t(e) \ge g(n,m)/v$ for an edge from $n$ t
          +o $m$, because $r(e)\ge 0$. Adding                            
      142 +the triangle inequality $g(n,B) \le g(n,m)+g(m,B)$ gives $h(n)
          + \le c_\lambda(e) + h(m)$, so the                             
      143 +heuristic is consistent and A* returns a route of minimum cost
          +~\cite{russell2021}. With                                     
      144 +$\lambda=0$ the search returns the fastest route, our baseline
          +.

      146 +\textbf{Time budget.} For a trip with fastest time $T_0$ and b
          +udget $\varepsilon$, we return the                            
      147 +route $P_\lambda$ for the largest $\lambda$ with $T(P_\lambda)
          +\le(1+\varepsilon)T_0$. To justify                            
      148 +searching over $\lambda$, take $\lambda_1<\lambda_2$ with opti
          +mal routes $P_1$ and $P_2$. Since                             
      149 +each route is optimal for its own $\lambda$,                  
      150 +$T_1+\lambda_1 R_1 \le T_2+\lambda_1 R_2$ and $T_2+\lambda_2 R
          +_2 \le T_1+\lambda_2 R_1$. Adding                             
      151 +the two inequalities gives $(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)(R_2-R_1)\le 
          +0$, so $R_2\le R_1$, and then the                             
      152 +first inequality gives $T_2 \ge T_1$. A larger $\lambda$ never
          + increases risk and never decreases                           
      153 +time, so the feasible values of $\lambda$ form an interval sta
          +rting at 0, and bisection finds its                           
      154 +end. We bisect $\log\lambda$ over $[10^{-2},10^{4}]$ in 16 ste
          +ps.
